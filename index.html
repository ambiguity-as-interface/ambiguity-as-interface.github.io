About

Not every possibility needs to become a decision.

Creators discover what they want as they create. Generative AI can expand this exploration but also fills in details creators would rather leave open. How can interfaces let creators indicate what is provisional, what remains open, and what they are ready to commit to? We treat ambiguity as a feature of interaction that creators can express and adjust. Bringing together creative practitioners and researchers in HCI, machine learning, cognition, and creativity, we connect four questions: how to capture tentative interests and deferred decisions, how to represent and model them, how creators manage commitment and revise system interpretations, and how to evaluate support as intentions evolve. In two in-person sessions, participants across creative domains identify where current tools encourage premature commitment, then prototype interactions that let creators keep decisions open, tentative, and revisable. We aim to develop a design space and shared research agenda for ambiguity-aware generative authoring.

Read the motivation & research background +

In creative workflows, creators develop and refine their goals through exploration. They gather references, generate and compare alternatives, keep promising results while trying other directions, and return to earlier ideas. Throughout this process, problems and solutions develop together (Dorst & Cross, 2001). Representations that allow multiple interpretations can also provide resources for exploration (Gaver et al., 2003). Leaving decisions open is therefore a working state in which creators can develop a direction through further experience and judgment.

Generative AI can broaden this exploration by rapidly producing alternatives, yet keeping decisions open presents a challenge. Prompt-based interfaces ask creators to put their intentions into words, even when those intentions are difficult to express or still forming (Chung & Adar, 2023; Kreminski & Chung, 2024; Wen et al., 2026). When details remain unspecified, generation draws on learned model priors to produce concrete outputs. These outputs may appear complete even where the creator has not made a decision. They can also redirect attention toward evaluating and elaborating suggested ideas before creators have fully developed their own direction (Bhat et al., 2026). Unexpected results can offer productive surprise that opens new directions (Son et al., 2024), but they can also introduce misalignment or encourage design fixation and premature convergence (Kreminski & Chung, 2024; Wadinambiarachchi et al., 2024). Empirical findings reflect this range of possibilities: studies report preference discovery (Wen et al., 2026) and qualitative reports of stronger agency and ownership (Fu et al., 2026), as well as reduced diversity across outputs (Doshi & Hauser, 2024). Taken together, these findings motivate examining when AI suggestions expand or narrow exploration across domains, creative stages, and interaction methods (Kim & Kim, 2026). How creators perceive these contributions also matters. Making interaction histories visible can help others understand how human and AI contributions developed (Siddiqui et al., 2026), yet making AI contributions explicit can also unsettle creators’ sense of authorship (Hu et al., 2026). This raises the question of whether helping creators distinguish what they requested from what the model introduced changes their later judgments and sense of authorship (Kim et al., 2026).

Interactions beyond prompt writing offer ways to address this challenge. Creators can refine requirements and explore ideas through questions and answers (Wen et al., 2026; Yuan et al., 2022), develop directions through visual comparison and selection (Son et al., 2024), adapt narrative strategies through manipulable representations (Zhang et al., 2026), and express desired changes through direct editing (Chung & Adar, 2023; Choi et al., 2025). However, the same action can convey different intentions. Selecting a draft may express an established preference, tentative interest in one feature, or a wish to explore further. Likewise, difficulty expressing an existing intention and discovering an intention through making may require different forms of support (Kreminski & Chung, 2024). Preference elicitation and preference learning provide ways to turn comparisons and edits into computational models (Koyama et al., 2014; Koyama et al., 2016). Yet, when an approach assumes preferences already exist and need to be uncovered, it may struggle to distinguish uncertainty about a preference from the formation of a new one (Wen et al., 2026). This raises questions about how models should represent deferred decisions and changes in direction while retaining earlier context. Preserving relations among expressed ideas can help systems interpret subsequent input in context (Choi & Hyun, 2026). A shift may also reflect an analogy or personal association that similarity between artifacts alone does not reveal. Capturing these connections remains a modeling question for creativity support tools.

The central question is how to design interactions in which creators’ expressions and systems’ interpretations shape one another. Addressing these questions requires connecting different perspectives on ambiguity. Computational approaches to intent elicitation often seek to reduce uncertainty about what users want (Wen et al., 2026), while design research also examines how openness to interpretation can support engagement and exploration (Gaver et al., 2003). These concerns intersect across four connected design questions: how creators’ judgments become observable signals (Capture), how models represent and interpret those signals (Represent/Model), how creators adjust what to specify and what to leave open (Interact), and how we assess whether this support benefits creative work (Evaluate). Choices in each area shape the others. Feedback interfaces affect judgment accuracy and cognitive effort (Shi et al., 2025), while the way models guide exploration can affect sense of agency and expressiveness (Chan et al., 2022; Koyama & Goto, 2022). Evaluation must also account for changes in direction and judgment criteria that short studies with fixed goals may not capture. It needs to distinguish agency over the process, authorship of the outcome, and credit attributed to AI. These connections call for shared ways of discussing which forms of ambiguity to maintain or resolve, how to capture and model evolving intent, and how to evaluate the resulting support. Evaluation should also account for changes in direction and judgment criteria that short studies with fixed goals may not capture, as well as changes in self-efficacy and trust that shape how creators delegate work to AI over successive interactions (Park et al., 2026).

This workshop brings together creative practitioners and researchers in HCI, machine learning, cognition, and creativity to develop these shared perspectives through collaborative prototyping. Building on work that supports flexible exploration and refinement (Kim et al., 2026), groups will work with concrete creative situations to prototype interactions that let creators adjust what to specify, leave decisions open, and revisit earlier choices. Making prototypes together will help participants examine assumptions about user behavior against creative experience and connect methods from different fields through concrete design decisions. Comparing the prototypes, their design rationales, and the phenomena they seek to support will also provide a basis for connecting knowledge across creativity support tools (Frich et al., 2019). Through these activities, we aim to develop a design space and shared research agenda for generative authoring that responds to ambiguity and evolving intent.

Call for participation

Submit a position paper describing relevant work, a creative experience, or an open question. In interdisciplinary groups, we will share experiences and build interaction prototypes, then compare designs and discuss how to evaluate their support for exploration.

  • Prototypes can be sketches, storyboards, enacted scenarios, or demos.
  • AI-assisted coding is optional; technical support and starter templates will be provided.
  • All CHI attendees are welcome. Authors of accepted papers receive priority.
  • With authors’ consent, accepted papers will appear on this website.

03

Program

From shared challenges to possible interfaces.

The workshop will comprise two 90-minute sessions, separated by a break, for 20–40 participants. The first session will identify challenges across creative domains; the second will explore responses through collaborative design and prototyping. Accepted position papers, activity instructions, and starter templates will be shared at least one week beforehand.

Proposed program · Select an activity for details

01 / Identify the challenges

90 MINUTES
5 MINOpening+

Organizers will introduce the workshop goals and its four themes: Capture, Represent/Model, Interact, and Evaluate. We will outline how the activities contribute to a design space and research agenda for supporting ambiguous and evolving intent.

30 MINInvited Talks+

We will invite an expert to give a 25-minute talk on ambiguity in creative practice and generative systems, followed by five minutes of Q&A. Drawing on examples from research and practice, the talk will introduce challenges and opportunities concerning creative agency, representations of evolving intent, and evaluation, providing starting points for the subsequent group activities.

25 MINPosition-Paper Introductions+

Representatives from accepted position paper teams will informally introduce their contributions, with around two minutes per team. In a conversational format without slides, participants will briefly describe their creative domain, the topic of their position paper, and a question they hope to explore during the workshop. Organizers will record emerging themes and challenges on a shared board to inform topic formation and subsequent group discussions.

5 MINTopic Formation+

Organizers will refine the candidate topics using the challenges on the shared board and present four to six topics. Participants will join the topic group of their choice, with groups capped at six members. Organizers will encourage each group to include participants from design, HCI, and AI/ML backgrounds. These groups will continue through both sessions.

30 MINGroup Work: Identifying Challenges+

Within their topic groups, participants will discuss when current interfaces constrain exploration or encourage premature commitment, drawing on the position papers and their own practice. Groups will distinguish intentions that are difficult to articulate from decisions deliberately left open, considering both individual and collaborative authoring. Each group will document a concrete scenario, the limitations of existing tools, and an opportunity for interface design. The final 10 minutes will be devoted to sharing observations across groups and identifying challenges that cut across topics.

BREAK

02 / Prototype the possibilities

90 MINUTES
50 MINGroup Work: Ideation and Prototyping+

Topic groups will continue into ideation, each building on one challenge identified in Session 1. Teams will design new forms of interaction—using sketching, spatial arrangement, sound, or multimodal combinations—that let creators express partial intentions, keep multiple possibilities open, and change direction without fully articulating a desired outcome. After 15 minutes of ideation, teams will spend 40 minutes developing their concepts as sketches, storyboards, enacted scenarios, or prototypes. AI-assisted coding (e.g., "vibe coding") is optional; organizers will provide starter templates, model/API credits, and technical support. Each team will produce an annotated storyboard or short slide deck that walks through a creative episode in which an intention stays unresolved, shifts through exploration, or develops in response to an unexpected AI contribution. The deck will also note what the system must represent about that intention (e.g., pending decisions or kept alternatives) and one open research question with a possible evaluation approach.

40 MINSharing, Reflection, and Synthesis+

Teams will briefly present their interaction concepts, open research questions, and possible evaluation approaches. As teams share, organizers will record emerging ideas and cluster related challenges on a shared board. Building on these contributions, participants will discuss how the proposed interactions might preserve ambiguity, support evolving intent, or introduce new constraints, comparing perspectives across domains and individual or collaborative settings. Participants will refine these clusters into a preliminary map of design opportunities and identify a small set of priority research questions across the four workshop themes. The session will conclude with directions for follow-up collaboration. Organizers will subsequently consolidate the workshop outputs and discussion into a design space and shared research agenda.

Groups of up to six participants continue across both sessions. Each team develops an annotated storyboard or short slide deck, an open research question, and a possible evaluation approach.
